Hypeline · hover

Ten ways, none of them a fill

Three rounds have all been variations on changing the surface colour, and none of them landed. These ten mostly leave the surface alone and answer the pointer some other way — with an edge, a mark, a movement, a light, or the type itself. Hover each strip. The theme button matters, as always.

1

The silk edge lights up

the app's own ring, on demand

The turning pastel ring is already the signature of this app — on the player, the moment chips, the rail cards. Here it is invisible until the pointer arrives. Nothing moves and nothing fills; the thing simply becomes one of the special ones for as long as you are on it. The risk is that the ring already means "this matters" elsewhere.

2

The outline draws itself

two halves meeting at the top

An ink hairline grows out from the middle in both directions and closes around the element. Precise, quiet, and it reads as selection rather than decoration — the gesture a drafting tool would use. Costs nothing but a border.

3

A thread underneath

2 px of the hype palette

A rule in lilac → petal → apricot grows left to right under the element, like a tiny length of the hype thread. The lightest touch of the ten, and the most obviously ours. On a row it underlines the whole row; on a button it sits just below the baseline.

4

Slate brackets

corner marks, pulled outward

Two corner marks appear and push out a few pixels, the way a camera or an editing timeline frames a selection. For an app about cutting clips this is the most on-subject idea here — it says "this is the thing you are about to take".

5

It rises

2 px and a shadow, no colour at all

Pure elevation: the element lifts off the paper and casts a soft shadow. Already in the app on the moment chips and cards, so choosing it would mean one gesture everywhere instead of two. Completely colour-neutral, which after three rounds may be the point.

6

It presses in

the opposite of 5

The element shrinks very slightly, as if the pointer were pushing it into the page. Almost no one does this on hover — it is normally reserved for the click — which makes it feel unusually physical. Small controls take it better than wide rows.

7

Frost

the glass thickens

The app is built on frosted glass, so the hover makes the glass more itself: blurrier, a touch brighter, with a sharper highlight along the top edge. The surface changes without changing colour. It needs something behind it to blur, so it is strongest over the mesh and weakest inside a flat panel.

8

The pointer carries a light

follows the cursor

A soft round light sits under the pointer and moves with it across the element. The only one of the ten that responds to where you are, not just that you are — which makes a wide row feel alive in a way a uniform fill cannot. Needs two lines of JavaScript to feed the cursor position in.

9

A pastel of its own

sky, petal, butter, apricot, lilac

Each kind of control takes one of the five palette tints — rows sky, buttons petal, icons butter, chips apricot, links lilac. It is a fill, but never grey and never the same twice, and it teaches the palette by using it. The risk is obvious: five hover colours is four more than most systems have.

10

The type answers

weight, tracking, and an arrow

No surface, no edge, no movement of the box: the label thickens and opens up its letter spacing, and a small arrow slides in from the right. Editorial rather than interface — it suits the Gloock-and-Manrope side of this app. Rows and icons keep their tracking, since mono and single glyphs do not take it well.

Tell me a number, or two you would like crossed — several of these combine well (2 + 5, or 3 + 10). If none of them is right, say what is wrong with the closest one and the next ten will be better aimed.
